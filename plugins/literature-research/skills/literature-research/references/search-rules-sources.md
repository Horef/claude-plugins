## Source keys

- **Coch** Lefebvre et al., "Chapter 4: Searching for and selecting studies", Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions v6.5.1 (last updated March 2025). Cited by section number; "MECIR C19" etc. are the numbered expectations in its boxes.
- **PRS** Rethlefsen et al. 2021, "PRISMA-S: an extension to the PRISMA Statement for reporting literature searches in systematic reviews", Syst Rev, doi 10.1186/s13643-020-01542-z. Cited by checklist item (1 to 16). A reporting guideline, not a conduct guideline; its items are used as a list of what a log must hold.
- **P20** Page et al. 2021, "The PRISMA 2020 statement", BMJ, doi 10.1136/bmj.n71. Cited by item number.
- **Gar21** Garritty et al. 2021, "Cochrane Rapid Reviews Methods Group offers evidence-informed guidance to conduct rapid reviews", J Clin Epidemiol, doi 10.1016/j.jclinepi.2020.10.007. Cited as R1 to R26 (its Table 1) and by section. Interim guidance, partly consensus, partly evidence; it says itself that best practice is limited by the lack of evidence on some shortcuts. A reasonable default, not proof.
- **Hir** Hirt et al. 2023, "Citation tracking for systematic literature searching: a scoping review", Res Synth Methods, doi 10.1002/jrsm.1635. 47 methodological studies. Claims come from the preprint (doi 10.1101/2022.09.29.22280494); check them against the published version before quoting.
- **GP05** Greenhalgh & Peacock 2005, "Effectiveness and efficiency of search methods in systematic reviews of complex evidence: audit of primary sources", BMJ, doi 10.1136/bmj.38636.593461.68. One audit of 495 sources in one review (health-service innovation), not a general law.
- **JB15** Jergas & Baethge 2015, "Quotation accuracy in medical journal articles: a systematic review and meta-analysis", PeerJ, doi 10.7717/peerj.1364. 28 studies in the main analysis.
- **Gre09** Greenberg 2009, "How citation distortions create unfounded authority: analysis of a citation network", BMJ, doi 10.1136/bmj.b2680. One claim (in inclusion body myositis), 242 papers; the mechanisms generalise, the rates do not.
- **Tri14** Tripathy et al. 2014, "NeuroElectro: a window to the world's neuron electrophysiology data", Front Neuroinform, doi 10.3389/fninf.2014.00040. Cited by section.
- **Tri15** Tripathy et al. 2015, "Brain-wide analysis of electrophysiological diversity yields novel categorization of mammalian neuron types", J Neurophysiol, doi 10.1152/jn.00237.2015.
- **Pau13** Pautasso 2013, "Ten simple rules for writing a literature review", PLoS Comput Biol, doi 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1003149. Cited by Rule number. Experience-based advice from one author, not evidence; the weakest source here.

## Reasons and sources by rule

### A. Framing the question

- **A1.** A parameter lookup needs conditions-matched primary measurements, a prior-art check is a claim of absence, a claim check needs no search; the wrong procedure wastes the day or overstates the result. Source: Coch 4.4.2 (PICO unsuited to methods and other non-intervention questions; a "tailored approach"); Pau13 Rule 4 (choose the review type from the material and the time).
- **A2.** Without the decision there is no way to say what is enough, and the search expands to fill the day. Source: Gar21 R1 (refine the question and eligibility criteria with the users so the review is fit for purpose, and consult them about changes); Pau13 Rule 1 (a well-defined topic and audience).
- **A4.** Criteria written after seeing the papers drift toward the expected answer, and two papers can use one name for different protocols or calculations, so the definition is part of eligibility. Scope is the main time lever. The plan will change, since iterative searching is normal; what matters is that the change is visible. Source: Coch 4.3.1.1 MECIR C19 (searches are motivated directly by the eligibility criteria); P20 item 5; Pau13 Rule 2 (define exclusion criteria early) and Rule 5 (keep the review focused); Tri14 2.3.3 (spike amplitude from threshold or from resting potential); Gar21 R2 to R4 (limit interventions, comparators and outcomes to those most important for decisions) and R23 to R25 (a protocol first; post hoc changes allowed if documented); P20 item 24c (describe amendments).
- **A6.** Restrictions can drop studies and change the result, and shortcuts differ in cost by topic. A neuroscience parameter is often first measured in older papers (`[syn]`). Source: Coch 4.3.1.1 MECIR C19 (do not restrict by language or publication status); Gar21 R5 to R7 and section 3.3.2 (date limits can lose studies and change meta-analytic results; English only is a viable shortcut for conventional interventions, not where non-English studies are expected); PRS item 9; Tri14 (its discussion of extraction limits: older papers available only as scanned images could not be processed). Disagreement: Coch treats unrestricted searching as mandatory for Cochrane reviews; Gar21 allows justified restrictions for rapid reviews. Resolution: restrict only with a written reason and carry it into the claim.
- **A7.** Coch itself says deciding when a search is complete is hard and the stopping rules have had "little formal evaluation", so the safest course is to fix a rule and report it. Source: Coch 4.4.11 (no-new-records and precision cut-offs; do not rely only on finding known key papers, which may mean the search is biased toward them; citation searching and reference checking as checks of performance; scarce evidence calls for caution). Capture-recapture and relative recall are named there and not used here. Pau13 Rule 10 adds a late check of recent papers (B8).
- **A9.** Someone has probably reviewed the topic or a neighbour of it. Source: Pau13 Rule 2 (seek previous reviews; discuss their limits); Coch 4.3.5 MECIR C29 (search within previous reviews: highly desirable) and C30 (check reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews: mandatory).

### B. Search design

- **B1.** One database is a biased sample of the literature, and published, indexed papers are a biased subset of the work done. Source: Coch 4.2.2 (MEDLINE alone is inadequate; searching beyond it reduces selection bias), 4.3.1.1 (two or more databases lower the risk of missing studies; specialised or cross-disciplinary topics may need more) and 4.3.5 (grey literature, previous reviews, reference lists); Gar21 R10 and R11 (the core databases plus at most one or two specialised ones) and R13 (limit grey literature and supplementary searching in a rapid review); PRS items 3, 4, 6, 7 (registries, online sources, contacting experts, other methods).
- **B2.** OR gives sensitivity inside a concept, AND ensures each concept is present. Outcomes and comparators are often absent from titles and abstracts or poorly indexed, so putting every aspect into the query loses records; NOT can silently remove relevant ones. PICO is designed for interventions and fits methods, mechanisms and measurement questions poorly; for those a single Boolean string misses much. Source: Coch 4.4.2 and MECIR C32 (also: multi-stranded and iterative searches, citation searching on key articles); GP05 Discussion (complex evidence cannot rely only on predefined strategies).
- **B3.** Indexing is incomplete and inconsistent, and so is authors' wording; in neuroscience one quantity travels under several names. Term lists are built by iteration on the retrieved records. Source: Coch 4.4.4 and MECIR C33 (free text and controlled vocabulary; an "iterative process"); Tri15 Methods (common synonyms and abbreviations, such as "input resistance" often written "R_in"); Tri14 2.4 and 3.4 (neuron-type mapping fails when synonyms or acronyms are missing) and 2.7 (MeSH terms used for species, strain and preparation).
- **B4.** Reading titles and abstracts is cheap relative to the review, so a broad net is affordable; a narrow query is the commoner failure. Source: Coch 4.4.3 (sensitivity against precision; about 60 to 120 abstracts an hour) and Key Points. Disagreement: Coch assumes human abstract-reading time is cheap, while an agent pays in tokens and context, hence the tiers; and Pau13 Rule 5 and Gar21 R2 to R4 want a narrow scope, hence broad in the query within a narrow question.
- **B7.** Field tags, thesauri, truncation and proximity differ between databases. Source: Coch MECIR C33 (strategies customised for each database).
- **B8.** A log reconstructed afterwards is "nearly impossible", and a reader cannot judge a review without the exact strings; a reviewer of the search also needs the reasons behind term choices; a search ages. Source: Coch 4.5 and MECIR C36 (sources, dates, terms; save strategies as run; retyping introduces errors; web results are not reproducible, so keep copies; the search narrative describes the major decisions); PRS items 1, 4, 8, 9, 12, 13, 15; P20 item 6; Pau13 Rule 10 (papers in press; search again at revision stage).
- **B10.** Errors in published search strategies are common, and a checklist-based review improves search quality, in rapid reviews too. PRESS was not read, so the checklist items are ours. Source: Coch 4.4.8 (peer review strongly recommended before running; PRESS); PRS item 14; Gar21 R12 (peer review of at least one strategy, for example MEDLINE). Disagreement: Coch wants it for every search, Gar21 for at least one; resolution: the main source's query.
- **B12.** Personal knowledge and contacts supplied about a quarter of the sources in the audit, at the lowest time cost. Source: GP05 Results (23% of 495 sources known to or recommended to the team, "took little time"; 24% in Table 1, which adds serendipity and own networks).

### C. Citation chasing

- **C2.** It is cheap and was the most productive single method in the one audit that measured it. Source: Coch MECIR C30 (mandatory); GP05 Results (reference tracking supplied 44% of the 495 sources, the largest single yield, though its time cost could not be separated from appraisal).
- **C3.** Forward chasing finds recent papers and reviews that keywords miss; indexes complement each other; the benefit is not uniform. Source: GP05 Results (electronic forward tracking took about one day and found five reviews, three of them by no other method, and 12% of the empirical studies; roughly one useful paper per 15 minutes) and Discussion (complex, heterogeneous evidence; obscure locations); Hir Discussion (every study that used more than one index found that they complemented each other; Google Scholar had a high administrative and time cost; more benefit for topics without consistent terminology, less for clearly defined questions). Disagreement: Hir found added value in 43 of 45 studies that assessed it, warns that publication bias probably inflates this, and describes two studies where it added almost nothing (one eligible paper from forward chasing of 40 included papers); GP05 found chasing the strongest method, in one review of complex evidence; Coch 4.4.2 lists it as an option, apart from reference lists (C30). Resolution: backward always, forward on a handful of key seeds, more only while it pays.
- **C5.** Most reviews stop after one round; three of the studies that went further found papers only in the second or third round. Source: Hir Results and Discussion (the authors' own review needed three rounds).
- **C8.** Citations are biased toward supportive papers: in the one network analysed, papers conflicting with the claim were cited far less, and negative results rarely spread through later citations, so a chase that follows the cited papers inherits the bias. Source: Gre09 (citation bias against critical primary data; Discussion); P20 items 14 and 21 (risk of bias due to missing results).
- **C9.** A log that says "snowballing" cannot be repeated or checked; terminology in the field is heterogeneous. Co-cited (shares citing papers with the seed) and co-citing (shares references with it) chasing exist as well and are not used here. Source: Hir Introduction (definitions) and Discussion (reporting of chasing is unclear and not standardised); PRS item 5 (state whether cited or citing references were examined, the methods and the base articles); Coch 4.5 (plan how to record forward chasing).

### D. Screening

- **D1.** Source: Coch 4.6.3 (merge, remove duplicates, examine titles and abstracts "over-inclusive" at this stage); PRS items 15 and 16 (record totals and the deduplication method).
- **D2.** A paper can be eligible or not depending on the preparation or the definition. Source: Coch 4.6.4 and MECIR C39 (inclusion decisions on full texts where possible, after an initial screen of titles and abstracts).
- **D3.** The same recordings appear in the original paper, a later paper and a review; counting each turns one measurement into three. In one network, 242 papers and over two hundred thousand citation paths supported a belief whose primary support was a few small papers, with the critical papers largely ignored. Source: Coch 4.6.2 and MECIR C42 (collate multiple reports; choose and justify the primary report; keep the secondary ones); Gre09 Results and Discussion (information cascade; unsound cascades may persist).
- **D5.** An excluded relevant paper costs more than an included irrelevant one, and one screener's errors are not visible to that screener. Source: Coch 4.6.4 and MECIR C39 (two independent people for inclusion decisions; single screening of titles and abstracts is acceptable; a second screener who is not a content expert is an advantage, since experts bring preformed opinions); Gar21 R14 and R15 (one screens, a second checks the excluded records). Disagreement: Coch asks for two independent people at the full-text stage and notes some studies find single screening adequate; Gar21 accepts one screener plus a check of the excluded ones. Resolution: one pass plus a fresh check of the exclusions.
- **D6.** It shows the criteria were applied and lets a reader challenge a decision. Source: Coch 4.6.5 (list excluded studies with the primary reason; keep it brief); P20 item 16b (cite studies that might appear to meet the criteria and explain why they were excluded).
- **D7.** Excluding on unusable data biases the result, and most neuroscience measurements sit in text and figures, which are harder to extract than tables. Source: Coch 4.6.3 MECIR C40; Tri14 3.5 (an estimated 5 to 10% of published electrophysiological data are in tables).
- **D8.** Source: Coch 4.6.3 (studies with incomplete or unobtainable data are tagged and listed as awaiting classification; ongoing studies are tagged).

### E. Extraction into the evidence ledger

- **E1.** Prose written from memory or from a skim mixes sources, drops conditions and cannot be checked. Source: Pau13 Rule 3 (take notes while reading; quotation marks for verbatim text; record references at once to avoid misattribution); P20 items 9 and 10.
- **E2.** Each field answers a question a later reader or a checker will ask. Authors do not always say how they computed a value, and pooled values then carry unexplained variance. Source: Tri14 2.7 (species, strain, electrode type, preparation type, junction-potential correction, age, temperature) and 2.3.3 (spike amplitude from threshold or from rest; input resistance and time constant not always defined); Tri15 Methods (mean, error term and replicates when possible; three-way junction-potential coding; control conditions only; time-constant values far from the median excluded because the definition varied); P20 items 10a and 10b.
- **E4.** A blank cannot be told from an oversight, and a guessed condition looks like a measured one. Source: Tri15 Methods (junction-potential correction coded as corrected, not corrected or not reported; its imputation was a model-fitting step, not a record of what the paper said); P20 item 10b.
- **E5.** Source: Tri15 Methods (weight converted to age with vendor tables; "room temperature" assigned 22 degrees C, in vivo 37 degrees C: stated by the authors as their conventions); P20 item 13b (describe data conversions).
- **E6.** A checker, or you next week, must open the same place; preprint and published numbers can differ, and a paper seen only as an abstract or a snippet has not been checked. Source: PRS item 5; JB15 Discussion (place the reference next to the statement it supports); Gre09 ("back door" invention: abstracts cited as peer-reviewed papers).
- **E7.** A number without its sentence cannot be checked and invites paraphrase drift. Source: Pau13 Rule 3; Tri14 2.7 (conditions come from the methods section, not the results table).
- **E8.** A number a paper reports as its own measurement differs from a number it cites; a belief can grow by citations to papers that hold no data on it. Source: JB15 Methods (indirect or secondary references: a review cited instead of the research article); Gre09 (amplification); Tri14 2.7 (preparation types include models).
- **E9.** Automated extraction of conditions was about 50% accurate on average and worse for continuous values such as age and temperature, so everything was checked by hand; this applies to a language model reading a PDF as much as to the rule-based pipeline tested there (`[syn]`). Source: Tri14 2.7 and 3.5 (5 to 10% of data in tables; scanned older papers were out of reach of their method).

### F. Verification

- **F1.** Quotation errors are common in the published literature. Pooled estimates, random effects: major errors 11.9% (95% CI 8.4 to 16.6), minor 11.5% (8.3 to 15.7), total 25.4% (19.5 to 32.4), I2 95% to 97%, 28 studies (27 for the major and minor rates), all in medical journals; the lowest total-error estimate of any study was 6.7%. Heterogeneity is very high, so a subfield may be better or worse. Citing a paper the citing authors may not have read is thought to be common (the authors cite an estimate, for physics and from another study, that 70 to 90% of citations are copied from reference lists). A claim written down earlier looks like fact when read again. Source: JB15 Results and Discussion (also the proposals: verify against original papers, not indirect sources; prefer original material over abstracts and narrative reviews); Pau13 Rule 9 (be objective about your own work); Gre09 (transmutation).
- **F3.** Beliefs spread by citation through reviews: in the one network studied, a statement went from hypothesis to fact through citations alone, and some citations pointed to papers that did not address the claim. Source: Gre09 Results (transmutation, dead-end citation, abstracts treated as papers); JB15 (indirect references).
- **F4.** The failure is often a shift of meaning, not an error, and a checker needs names to look for. Source: Gre09 (box "Vocabulary of citation distortions"; sections "Citation diversion" and "Invention"); the author notes that readers may interpret a text differently.
- **F5.** A fixed set makes verdicts countable and comparable across checkers; the recorded passage lets another reader disagree. Source: JB15 Methods (a major error "is not at all in accordance with the claim"; minor: partial; indirect references counted as minor by the studies that counted them) and Discussion (a source may support a statement in the letter and still be inadequate for it; studies applied different standards, which partly explains their heterogeneity). Disagreement: JB15 counts citing a review instead of the original as a minor error, Gre09 treats it as a route to amplification; resolution: `indirect` is its own verdict, and it is traced (F3).
- **F6.** The same quantity has different values across species, ages, preparations and temperatures. Experimental conditions explain "a substantial degree" of the variability in literature values, and accounting for them shows the data to be "far more reproducible across laboratories than previously appreciated", so many apparent conflicts are condition differences. Source: Tri15 abstract and Methods (control or normotypic conditions identified by manual curation); Tri14 2.7 (input resistance falls in neurons from older animals) and 2.3.3.
- **F7.** A unit slip or a mix of standard deviation and standard error changes a value by orders of magnitude or by the square root of n; properties such as input resistance vary over orders of magnitude between cell types, so plausibility needs the cell type's range. Source: P20 item 13b; Tri15 Methods (log-transformed properties; error term and replicate numbers often unavailable).
- **F10.** Whoever extracted a value reads the passage as they expect, and a checker that knows the expected answer inherits that. Extraction errors are common and a second reader finds them cheaply. Source: JB15 Discussion (authors may interpret papers as they would like); Gar21 R16, R17 and R19 (single extractor on a piloted form with a minimal set of data items; a second reviewer verifies extraction and judgements); Coch 4.6.4 (independent decisions reduce single-person bias).
- **F14.** Retracted and corrected papers circulate and are cited. Source: Coch 4.4.6 and MECIR C48 (examine retraction statements and errata; reviews were often not corrected when included studies were retracted).

### G. Synthesis

- **G1.** It stops prose from carrying claims that never passed through the ledger; an unchecked claim that reaches the final text with a citation looks verified. The person asked in order to decide something. Source: Pau13 Rule 3; JB15 and Gre09 (claims acquire authority through citation alone); Gar21 section 3.2 (reviews serve timely decision-making). The id tagging is `[syn]`.
- **G4.** A spread of reported values is often a spread of conditions; averaging across species, ages or protocols gives a number nobody measured. Source: Tri15 abstract; Gar21 R21 (meta-analysis only when studies are similar enough to pool; narrative synthesis otherwise); P20 items 13a, 13c, 13e, 17 and 20c (tabulate; explore causes of heterogeneity).
- **G5.** A better-cited claim is not a better-supported one. Source: Gre09 Discussion; P20 item 13f (sensitivity analyses); JB15 Methods (leave-one-out analysis).
- **G6.** The label tells the reader how far to trust a conclusion without reading the ledger. GRADE itself was not adapted. Source: Gar21 R22 (grade certainty with a second reviewer verifying); P20 items 15 and 22; Pau13 Rule 6 (a review is critical, not a list: what is achieved, what is debated, what is open).
- **G7.** A gap is a result, and a gap with a cost is more useful than a general call for more data; a literature can look more consistent than the facts because negative results are published and cited less. Source: P20 items 14, 21, 23b and 23c; Pau13 Rule 6 and Rule 10 (do not call something a gap if papers just appeared or are in press); Gre09 Discussion; Hir Discussion (methodological studies with negative findings are probably under-published).

### H. Rapid versus systematic

- **H2.** A systematic review is defined by a pre-specified protocol, a search of several sources aimed at completeness, independent duplicate selection and extraction, appraisal and full reporting; a rapid review streamlines or omits some of these to answer in time. A one-day agent review is a rapid review at best, and its shortcuts are acceptable only if stated. Compared with database searching, chasing from a few seeds rarely found as many relevant papers. Source: Coch 4.2.2, 4.4.11 and 4.6.4; Gar21 section 3.2 and Table 1 (R10, R11, R13, R16, R17, R21); Pau13 Rule 4; Hir Discussion (stand-alone chasing is not sensitive enough for systematic or scoping reviews).
- **H3.** The credibility of a rapid review depends on its transparency about the shortcuts; no PRISMA extension for rapid reviews existed at the time of the guidance. Machine screening can cut the work by 30% to over 90%, sometimes at up to 5% lower sensitivity, and Coch (text from mid-2023) found no large, valid evaluations of language-model screening. Source: Gar21 (reporting section after R26); PRS items 1 to 16; P20 items 7, 8, 9, 16b, 23c, 27; Coch 4.6.6.2.
- **H4.** Database searches miss relevant records, and an absence claim is only as strong as the search behind it. Source: Coch 4.2.2 and 4.4.11; GP05 (protocol-driven searching supplied 30% of the sources in a complex review).
- **H6.** A rapid review fits a time-limited decision. Cochrane advises working with an information specialist or librarian from the outset; ask the person whether one is available. Source: Pau13 Rule 4; Coch Key Points and 4.2.1; Gar21 section 3.2.
